Creating Transparent PNGs with Background Removal

If your sheet is filled with one flat green or magenta, that colour can be keyed out and you get real transparency. Here is how the detection works, how to tune the tolerance, and what to do when the result disappoints you.

What background removal actually does

Background removal here is deterministic pixel maths. The tool picks one reference colour, measures the RGB distance from every pixel to that colour, and drives the alpha value to zero where the distance is small enough. No model is involved and nothing is estimated: the same input always yields the same output on any device.

That approach works when one condition holds: the target colour must be a solid colour that never appears inside the artwork. Magenta and fluorescent green are the usual choices because nothing in game art uses them. If the sprite has blue sky, red hair, or white eyes, those colours cannot be told apart from the background, and you get holes where you did not want them.

Worth knowing Background removal is available in the image cutter's background section and in the GIF splitter's solid background removal switch. It is not applied to batch cropping results, and it is not something you need when your source is already an alpha PNG.

Step 1. Load an image with a solid background

Load the sheet in the image cutter from the sprite sheet tool. The reference colour comes from sampling the image border. The image cutter reads a thin strip along the edges; the GIF splitter samples 8% of the border area of the first frame and then reuses that single colour for every frame.

That word "border" carries the weight. If the sprite sits in the middle and the entire surrounding area is one colour, the border is pure background and the reference is reliable. If artwork crosses the edge, the sampled colour can be wrong. And if the border is already transparent, there is nothing to key at all.

Step 2. Turn on background removal

Flip the "background removal" switch in the background section, described as detecting a solid background automatically and making it transparent. Detection starts immediately and the canvas updates.

If the image cannot be understood, you get "배경색을 감지하지 못했습니다. 배경이 단색인지 확인해 주세요." instead. Nothing will change while you move the slider in that state, so check the source image for a gradient or texture first.

Step 3. Read the detected colour and tune tolerance

On success, a swatch appears as "detected background #xxxxxx". Compare it against what you see. A mismatch means the reference itself is wrong, so toggle off and on again, or fix the source.

Next, adjust the "tolerance (similar colour removal range)" slider. Think of it as how far from the reference colour a pixel may be and still be deleted. Compression artefacts mean a supposedly flat area is never perfectly flat, and a wider tolerance sweeps up that variation. The range is 0% to 30% in 30 steps, with 10% as the default, and results refresh after a short 200ms debounce, so a brief pause before the canvas updates is normal.

What a wider tolerance costs you

Push tolerance too far and the sprite itself starts disappearing, beginning with shadows and outlines painted in colours close to the background. Keep it too tight and leftover specks stay behind, producing a dirty fringe. For a sheet whose background runs unbroken to the edge, 2% to 5% is often enough.

Step 4. Verify the result

The canvas shows the image at 100% with a checkerboard behind transparency, so wherever the pattern appears, pixels became transparent. Check three things: the surrounding area turned into checkerboard, the sprite silhouette has no holes punched through it, and any semi-transparent edge pixels look like the softness you intended.

That last point deserves care. Pixels within tolerance may not be deleted outright; their alpha can drop instead, leaving a background tint and a stepped-looking edge. The tool does not refine edges for you, so a cleaner source background is the real fix when this happens.

Step 5. Check the frames after splitting

Set the grid counts, cut, and open the result preview. Thumbnails sit on the same checkerboard, so the transparency is easy to judge frame by frame.

Removal applies once to the whole image. Frame 1 and frame 7 cannot receive different reference colours. If one sheet mixes several background colours, split it into two images, treat them separately, then recombine the results.

Step 6. Export transparent PNGs

Press save and download (ZIP), then "download (ZIP)" in the dialog. Output is always RGBA PNG; there is no JPG or WebP option, because those formats do not keep a usable alpha channel. Files are named after the source plus the grid, such as {filename}_{H}_{V}_1.png.

When you are unsure, flip the switch off. The canvas returns to the pre-processing image exactly, with the original pixels intact, so you can try values without risk.

The GIF tolerance cannot go below 1%, since anything lower has no practical meaning. A slim progress bar appears while frames are reprocessed, so you can keep dragging the slider instead of releasing it and waiting. For the GIF workflow around this switch, see converting a GIF into a sprite sheet.

Recommended order of operations

Background removal usually happens alongside other work, so a fixed order keeps the result stable.

  1. Confirm the image really has a solid background whose edge colour stays consistent all the way around.
  2. Strip the outer transparent gutter with padding first. Cleaning the padding before keying gives a more accurate reference colour.
  3. Turn on removal and compare the detected background colour against what you see.
  4. Start the tolerance at 2% to 5% and raise it only when needed.
  5. Set the grid counts and inspect transparency frame by frame in the result preview.
  6. Export RGBA PNGs with save and download (ZIP).

Skipping step 2 is technically fine, but the sprite must sit exactly inside the region for the colour comparison to be clean. Tidying the padding first makes a good result far more likely in one pass.

When you do not need removal at all

If your source is already an alpha PNG, do nothing: cutting preserves the transparency. There is also no need when your engine handles the colour itself, for instance with multiply blending or a custom keying shader. In that case cutting the original JPG keeps more quality than re-exporting through a keyed pass.

If transparency really is required, turn removal on and continue with the save steps in the basic cutting guide. To adjust individual frame positions afterwards, read per-frame editing and undo.
